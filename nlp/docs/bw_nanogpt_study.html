Causal BW on nanoGPT: what works

shakespeare_char, B = 64, T = 256, 6 layers, C = 384, dropout 0, 5000 steps, 1 GPU. All BW runs use the causal fix (bw_renorm=True, shared statistics). Feature list and pre-fix work: bw_paper_vs_code.html; the fix and GPT-2: bw_causal_report.html.

Summary. Causal BW gives a clear, seed-robust gain over LayerNorm on nanoGPT, larger than before the leak fix.
  • No dropout: 15% lower mean train loss and 7% lower final train loss on every seed; loss 0.1 is reached 6% sooner. Validation is unchanged. Before this study the causal version gave 8%; the leaky one 13%.
  • Vanilla nanoGPT (dropout 0.2): 4% lower mean and 10% lower final train loss on every seed, validation tied. Over 10k steps the final gain grows to −18% (2 seeds). Before the fix the best BW setting was 2% ahead, with the leak.
  • Recipe: renorm with shared statistics, running_mean, full width (group 384), no warmup, no gradient boost, BW on every MLP input followed by a LayerNorm without gain or bias (applied inside BW), and a higher learning rate (BW 1.5e-3; LayerNorm's best is 1e-3). No dropout: momentum 0.7, fix_factor 0.8. Dropout 0.2: more history and shrinkage, momentum 0.3, fix_factor 0.4, lr 2e-3.
  • Cost: 1.13× per iteration (15.7 against 13.9 ms, down from 27.7 with exact speedups). With the same hyperparameters for both, BW reaches LayerNorm's final loss in 10–13% fewer steps, so in wall-clock time it is about even (−2% to +1%). Whitening with ns_chol (Newton–Schulz forward, Cholesky backward) needs fewer steps at the same cost: −4.7% wall-clock time against +0.1% for Cholesky (3 seeds each), see bw_ns_chol.html.
  • GPT-2 (124M, 5000 steps): BW is ahead at every step but only by 0.03–0.04 train loss (about 7% fewer steps), at both lr 6e-4 and 1.2e-3; at 1.14× per iteration it does not yet win in wall-clock time.
  • Dropped: calibrated (ties LayerNorm at 2.5–7× the cost), warmup, gradient boost (diverges above 1), BW on the attention input, BW without the following LayerNorm.

Commands: --normalization_type=full_bw --bw_renorm=True --bw_renorm_stats=batch --batch_center_mode=running_mean --bw_group_size=384 --bw_warmup=none --bw_momentum=0.7 --bw_fix_factor=0.8 --learning_rate=1.5e-3 (dropout 0.2: --bw_momentum=0.3 --bw_fix_factor=0.4 --learning_rate=2e-3). About 2.9 GPU-hours for 61 runs, using CUDA MPS to run 4–6 per GPU.

What makes BW work: ablations of the best recipe

Each row removes one ingredient from the best recipe; the gradient variants change only the backward pass, the forward rule is the same. Condition number: geometric mean over the 6 layers.

  • All three ingredients are needed: decorrelation (ff > 0), shrinkage (ff < 1) and the gradient through the batch covariance. Remove any one and BW is worse than LayerNorm.
  • The covariance gradient is the core. Forward whitening alone (no statistics gradient) is unstable and diverges; the mean gradient alone is +17%. The covariance gradient removes from the backward pass the directions the whitening normalizes away, keeping the layer invariant to how the residual stream scales and mixes its channels.
  • Better conditioning is not the goal. Exact whitening (ff 1) gives the best-conditioned MLP input (300 at step 1000, against 908 for the best recipe and 2,616 for LayerNorm) and trains worst of the completed runs. Shrinkage compresses the spectrum without flattening it, so the relative strength of the directions survives.

Do we need the LayerNorm after BW?

Token-norm spread: standard deviation / mean of the per-token norms at the BW layer's output. The current setting was run twice (identical configuration, GPU nondeterminism): −13.3% and −14.4%, so differences near 1% are noise.

  • Yes, a per-token normalization is needed: BW alone only matches LayerNorm (+1.9%; +6.1% at lr 1e-3). With one after it, BW gains 14%.
  • Only its per-token scaling matters. RMSNorm (no centering) and LayerNorm without gain and bias do as well as the full LayerNorm. Centering is redundant (BW already centers) and so are the gains (BW has its own γ).
  • Why: the two normalize along different axes. BW applies one linear map to every token, from statistics over the batch: it decorrelates channels but cannot equalize token sizes, since a linear map keeps large tokens large. The MLP (GELU) is not scale-invariant, so tokens of different size see different effective gains. Without the per-token normalization the MLP input is BW's output, whose token norms spread by 23% at step 250, and training is slowest exactly there (loss 1.0 at step 1242 against 983); with it, every MLP input token has the same norm by construction. The per-token normalization also makes the sublayer exactly invariant to its input's scale at the current step, which BW's buffer statistics, one step stale, cannot.
  • Order matters: LayerNorm → BW (−9.8%) is worse than BW → LayerNorm, because BW after the LayerNorm brings the token-size spread back (27% at step 250).
  • Simplification: BW → RMSNorm or BW → LayerNorm without affine trains as well with fewer parameters.

Condition number (largest / second-smallest eigenvalue; LayerNorm output always has one null direction) of the MLP input covariance, one training batch, eval mode. Layers 1–5: geometric mean.

  • BW conditions the MLP input much better early on: 10–30× better than LayerNorm at step 250 and 4–14× at step 1000 (layers 1–5). By step 5000 LayerNorm has caught up, and the best recipe, with its higher learning rate, ends worse conditioned than LayerNorm while training best.
  • Layer 0 stays very ill-conditioned (103–106) in every model: its input is the token plus position embedding of a 65-symbol vocabulary, and the whitening shrinkage (fix_factor) caps how much BW can lift its smallest directions.
  • As a predictor it works early and within one knob. Across 80 runs, the rank correlation between MLP-input condition number and mean train loss is +0.74 at step 250 and -0.01 at step 5000, matching a gain that comes from early training. Within group size and fix_factor, worse conditioning means worse loss (group 128: about 105 at step 250, the worst completed BW run). Across modes and learning rates it misleads: leave_one_out conditions best and trains worse than running_mean.

Figure 3: Learning curves, dropout 0.2, 10k steps. Eval-mode train loss (solid; dropout off during eval) and validation loss (dashed), mean over seeds. The gap opens in the second half, as the learning rate decays, and settles at about −18%; LayerNorm at lr 2e-3 tracks LayerNorm at 1e-3. BW (best) reaches LayerNorm's final train loss (0.213) at step 8417, 16% fewer steps (1.19× faster per step; at 1.13× the cost per step, about 0.95× LayerNorm's wall-clock time).

  • The longer run doubles the final gain: −18% final train loss on both seeds (0.175 against 0.213), against −10% at 5k. Loss 0.3 is reached about 600 steps sooner.
  • Most of the gain comes while the learning rate decays. The gap is −1 to −2% for the first 4000 steps, then grows to −17% by step 8000 and holds. The absolute gap peaks around step 7000–8000 (0.047) and shrinks slightly by 10k (0.038), so an even longer run is unlikely to widen the relative gain much.
  • It is not the learning rate: LayerNorm at 2e-3 ends level with LayerNorm at 1e-3 (0.213).
  • Validation: the best validation loss is tied (about 1.46 for all). After that, every model overfits; BW overfits more (final val 2.38 against 2.27).

  • BW is ahead at every step, but by little: −0.24 at step 500, a steady −0.03 from step 2000. Unlike nanoGPT with dropout, the gap does not grow while the learning rate decays.
  • Both tolerate twice the learning rate and gain about 0.06 from it; BW gains slightly more (gap 0.036 against 0.030).
  • Per step BW wins (about 7% fewer steps); per GPU-hour it still loses, because it costs 1.14× per iteration. Cutting that cost is the next lever.
