Batch Whitening: paper vs. nanoGPT implementation

Paper: nlp/bw_paper (BW for CNNs, Causal BW). Code: nlp/nano_gpt/model.py, train.py. Setup: shakespeare_char, B = 64, T = 256, C = 384, 6 layers, no dropout.  Jump to experiments and conclusions ↓  Jump to dropout 0.2 experiments ↓  Jump to gradient-boost experiments ↓

Done: gradient-boost sweep (results). Open follow-ups:
  • Done: calibrated now defaults to leave-one-out centering (bw_calib_center=loo); the old blended-mean centering (blend) makes it run away.
  • Confirm the dropout gain from boost 0.5 at GPT-2 scale.

Summary of differences

  • Group size. The code's 16 comes from a hardcoded batch shape (B = 32, T = 64). The paper's rule on the real shape gives 48.
  • Momentum. Fixed at 0.1. The paper's adaptive rule is not implemented; at group size 16 it would give 1.0, i.e. leave-one-out.
  • Correlated-channel correction. The code uses ε = 0.1; the paper says ε close to 0.
  • Whitening matrix. The code uses Cholesky L−1, not Σ−1/2 as the paper states.
  • Placement. BW → LayerNorm → MLP, with no BW before attention. The paper places BW directly before the linear layer.
  • Mode-specific gaps. running_mean warmup leaves the covariance history 90% diagonal at handoff; leave_one_out inference uses the last training batch's statistics.
  • Not in the paper yet. Calibration pass, gradient boost, covariance warmup, calibrated covariance centering.

Experiments and conclusions

Question: does fixing the paper/code differences make BW beat the no-dropout LayerNorm baseline (l965qcs2) in training convergence? 35 full 5000-step runs on 1 GPU each (wandb names exp1-*, exp2-*, exp3-*).

Conclusions

  • BW beats the baseline. leave_one_out + full-channel whitening (group 384) + fix_factor 0.8: 15–22% lower train loss through step 2000, about even at steps 2500–3250, 3–5% lower at the end. Holds on all 3 seeds.
  • Group size is what matters. The paper's error budget (emax = 0.1) is far too conservative: group 384 has e ≈ 2.1 per batch and trains best.
  • Little history is best. At large groups, running_mean improves as momentum rises toward 1 (= leave_one_out).
  • Keep shrinkage. ε = 0.1–0.2 works; the paper's ε → 0 is neutral at group 16 and unstable at group 384.
  • calibrated does not scale. It diverged at group 384 with the old blended-mean centering; with the new leave-one-out centering it trains but stays behind (see the gradient-boost section). It also costs ~7× run time.

Experiments with dropout 0.2

Question: does what helped at dropout 0 also help with nanoGPT's original dropout (0.2 on embeddings, attention, MLP and residual), against the LayerNorm baseline a9554bt7? 29 full 5000-step runs (wandb names drop1-*, drop2-*, drop3-*).

Conclusions

  • The dropout-0 gain does not carry over. The best dropout-0 setting (leave_one_out, group 384, fix_factor 0.8) is ~7% worse in mean loss here, on all 3 seeds.
  • BW at best ties the baseline. With strong shrinkage (fix_factor 0.2–0.4), mean loss is 1.001 vs 1.001, and best validation loss 1.4686 vs 1.4674: within seed noise.
  • Only an early lead remains. These runs reach loss 1.5 ~5% sooner (513 vs 538 steps), are even by step ~2000, and end ~2% higher in train loss.
  • The levers flip. Larger groups, higher momentum and weak shrinkage all hurt with dropout. Decorrelation must be kept small, but not zero (fix_factor 0 is worse).
  • Not an eval-statistics problem. Train-mode loss (dropout on, batch statistics) shows the same ranking, so optimization itself is slower, not just the stored statistics used at eval.
  • Likely cause: noisy covariance. Dropout noise makes the batch covariance estimate unreliable, so full decorrelation adds noise instead of conditioning the input well. Two follow-ups could test this: tie shrinkage to the noise level (strong early, weaker later, or scaled with the dropout rate), or remove dropout only on the MLP input, where BW sits.

Experiments with the gradient boost

Question: does scaling the gradient of the current-batch BW statistics (forward pass unchanged) improve on the best configurations above? New knob bw_grad_scale (0 = per-mode default: 1/momentum for calibrated, 1 otherwise). It scales the covariance gradient in every mode, and also the centering-mean gradient in calibrated. 23 full 5000-step runs (wandb names gb1-* … gb4-*).

Conclusions

  • Without dropout, no boost for running_mean or leave_one_out. Boost 1 (the true gradient) is best in every configuration. Any boost above 1 hurts, and 2 already makes the best group-384 configurations diverge. Boost 0.5 (tested on leave_one_out) also hurts.
  • With dropout, a damped boost helps a little. Boost 0.5 on leave_one_out, group 384, fix_factor 0.4 gives mean loss 0.981 (3 seeds) vs 1.001 for the baseline and 1.001 with boost 1, and reaches loss 0.8 ~4% sooner. This is the first BW setting that beats the dropout baseline on every seed. Best validation loss is unchanged (1.4634 vs 1.4674), and in train mode (dropout on) the gain shrinks to a tie: boost 0.5 mainly stops the late-training drift that boost 1 shows.
  • Blended-mean centering breaks calibrated; the boost is not the cause. With data and covariance centered about the blended mean (cdcba46, now bw_calib_center=blend), activations run away at every boost (input RMS 150–21,000 by step 5000 at group 16, mean loss 1.7–2.4). Reverting the covariance centering restores the September behaviour (0.696, stable). Fixed: the default is now bw_calib_center=loo.
  • With leave-one-out centering, the boost does what the theory says for calibrated. Boost 10 (= 1/momentum) beats boost 1: 0.605 vs 0.686 at group 16, the best calibrated result so far. It only ties plain leave_one_out at group 16 (0.605) at ~7× the cost. Group 384: mean loss 0.736, worse than the baseline (0.492) and plain leave_one_out (0.428); pooling 10 calibration batches does not substitute for the fresh full-gradient covariance.
